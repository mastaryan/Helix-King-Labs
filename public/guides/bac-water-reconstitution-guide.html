BAC Water & Reconstitution Guide

Peptides ship freeze-dried for stability. Here's what bacteriostatic water is and how reconstitution is done properly at the lab bench.

What is bacteriostatic water?

Bacteriostatic water ("BAC water") is sterile water containing 0.9% benzyl alcohol as a preservative. The benzyl alcohol inhibits bacterial growth, which is why labs prefer it over plain sterile water for multi-use vials — a reconstituted vial gets punctured repeatedly, and each puncture is a contamination opportunity.

The technique

  1. Prepare the workspace. Clean bench, gloves. Wipe the vial stopper and the BAC water port with alcohol and let them dry.
  2. Draw the diluent. Pull the desired volume of BAC water into a sterile syringe. Typical working volumes are 1–3 mL depending on vial size and target concentration.
  3. Add slowly down the wall. Inject the water gently against the inside wall of the vial — never blast it directly into the powder cake. Foaming denatures peptides.
  4. Swirl, don't shake. Roll the vial gently between your palms until fully dissolved. Shaking creates foam and shear stress.
  5. Refrigerate. A reconstituted vial lives at 2–8°C. See our storage guide for shelf-life expectations.

Common mistakes

  • Shaking the vial — the single most common way to ruin good material.
  • Injecting diluent straight into the powder — causes foaming and incomplete dissolution.
  • Using tap or non-sterile water — introduces contaminants and uncontrolled pH.
  • Reconstituting months of material at once — only dissolve what the work plan needs; dry powder keeps far longer.

Concentration math

Concentration is just mass divided by volume: a 10 mg vial reconstituted in 2 mL gives 5 mg/mL. Our research calculator handles the arithmetic if you'd rather not do it by hand.
